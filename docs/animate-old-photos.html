How to animate an old photo

Start from the best scan you can get and crop it to the person. Then ask for one small, natural movement, like a turn of the head, a smile or hair moving in a breeze, with the camera still. Five seconds is plenty. The bigger and longer the motion, the further the face drifts from the real person, so gentle wins.

Updated 1 October 2026

What photo animation actually does

An image-to-video model looks at the photo and imagines the next few seconds: how the head would turn, how the light would move across a cheek, what the wind would do to the hair. It's an informed guess, not a recording. That's worth remembering with family photos. The result can be lovely, but it isn't footage of the person, and it's kind to say so when you share it.

1. Start from the best scan you can get

The model can only animate the detail it can see. Scan the print itself rather than photographing it: 600 dpi is a good floor for small prints, more for tiny ones. Light it evenly with no glare, and avoid copies saved from social media, which are small and compressed. Scratches and dust animate along with everything else, so a light cleanup of the still first is worth it.

2. Crop to what matters

Crop so the person fills a good part of the frame, with a little space around them to move into. A face that's a small part of a wide shot has little detail to work from, and that's where smearing starts.

3. Ask for one small movement

Describe what should happen in plain words, and keep it to one or two things:

  • Works well: “She turns her head slightly toward the camera and smiles. A soft breeze moves her hair.”
  • Works well: “He looks up from the book and blinks. Static camera.”
  • Asks for trouble: “She laughs, waves, stands up and walks toward the camera.” Every extra action is more for the model to invent, and the face pays for it.

4. Keep the camera still

Ask for a static camera, a gentle handheld feel (the example above), or at most a slow push-in. When the camera really moves, the model has to invent the background as well as the person, and the photo stops feeling like the photo.

5. Make a few takes and keep the best

The same photo and the same words give a different clip each time. Make three or four and keep the one where the face stays truest. It's normal for one take in four to be the keeper.

Add sound, or don't

A little ambient sound, like wind, birdsong or a room's quiet, makes a clip feel alive. Phoenix Motion in the browser makes the sound in the same pass if you describe it at the end of the prompt. Leave out speech: a voice that wasn't theirs is the fastest way to make an old photo feel wrong.

What goes wrong, and what to do about it

  • The face changes. Ask for less movement, keep the head turn small, and make more takes.
  • Hands, teeth and glasses look odd. Models still struggle with these. Choose a pose where hands are out of frame, and a smile over an open laugh.
  • The picture goes soft or hazy partway through. Fast, few-step models do this sometimes. Another take usually fixes it.
  • Everything moves. Say what should stay still: “Static camera. The background stays still.”

Questions

Will the animated photo still look like the real person?

Usually, in a short clip with gentle motion. The model has only one photo to go on, so the more the face turns or changes expression, the more it has to guess. A slight turn and a smile stay close; laughing, talking or a full turn to profile drift further.

Can I animate a black and white photo?

Yes. A black and white photo animates the same way and stays black and white. Colourising is a separate step, best done on the still before you animate it if you want colour.

Does it work on group photos?

It animates the whole picture, so yes, but keep the motion small. Faces that are small in the frame have little detail to work from and can smear when they move. Cropping to one or two people gives the best result.
