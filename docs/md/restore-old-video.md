# How to restore old video, whatever it was shot on

Tapes, DVDs, film scans and the phone clips you shot ten years ago all go soft in different ways. Fix the damage in the right order and they can look good on a modern screen. Fix it in the wrong order and you mostly get sharper noise.

Updated 28 September 2026

## First, work out what's actually wrong

Most old footage has two or three of these at once, and each needs a different fix, so it's worth a minute of looking before you touch anything.

- **Combed edges on anything that moves.** That's interlacing, from tape, camcorders, DVDs and TV recordings.
- **Crawling speckle or a gritty veil.** Tape noise, or sensor noise from a small phone camera in poor light.
- **Blocks, smears and mushy faces.** Compression. Early phones, DVDs and old uploads all threw detail away to save space.
- **It's just small.** 480p or 720p looks soft on a 4K TV even when the recording itself is clean.
- **Grain.** On film scans, grain is part of the picture, not damage. Keep it.

## Then fix it in this order

1. **Start from the best copy you have.** A clean capture of the tape, the original file off the old phone, the DVD's own files. A clip that went through a messaging app or a social network was compressed again on the way, and that damage stacks.
2. **Deinterlace first, if the footage is interlaced.** Tape, camcorder, DVD and TV footage usually is. Undo it before anything else, or every later step sharpens the comb teeth.
3. **Go easy on denoising before you upscale.** Modern restoration models learned on noisy, compressed footage. Scrub it clean first and you strip the texture they work from, and faces come out waxy.
4. **Restore and upscale in one pass.** Rebuild the detail that compression and noise buried, at the new size, in a single step. Sharpening a plain resize afterwards mostly sharpens the damage.
5. **Test 30 seconds before the whole thing.** Pick a short section with a face, some motion and a dark corner. If that looks natural, run the rest with the same settings.
6. **Export at the size you will watch it.** 1080p suits most screens. Go to 4K for a big TV when the source is decent. Much past four times the original size you are mostly making bigger files.

## Old phone video

Phone clips from the early 2010s are the most common thing people want fixed, and often the most rewarding, because the subject is usually someone you know. They're progressive, so there's nothing to deinterlace, but they're small, compressed hard, and noisy anywhere the light was poor. Hunt down the original file if you can: the copy in your chat history or on a social site has been squeezed at least once more. ## Film scans

8mm and Super 8 transfers are usually progressive and far more detailed than tape, but grain is part of how film looks. A restore that smooths it away turns people into plastic. Clean up dust and flicker, keep the grain.

## DVDs, old uploads and screen recordings

These are mostly compression problems, and DVDs are often interlaced too. Old uploads, gameplay captures and webcam footage were fine on a 2012 monitor and fall apart on a 4K one, which is exactly the damage a restoration model is good at.

## What restoration can't do

It can't recover detail the camera never recorded. A shot that was out of focus stays out of focus, and a face that was six pixels wide can only be guessed at. A good restore gives you a clean, stable picture of what was there. Anything promising to make a 1995 tape look like it was shot yesterday is inventing the difference.

## Questions

### Can you really improve old, low-quality video?

Yes, up to a point. Noise, interlacing and compression damage can be cleaned up, and the detail they buried can be rebuilt, so the picture holds up on a modern screen. Detail that was never recorded can't come back.

### What resolution should I upscale old video to?

1080p for most screens. 4K is worth it for a big TV when the source is reasonably clean. From standard definition, going much past four times the original size mostly makes bigger files, not a better picture.

### Should I denoise before upscaling?

Lightly, if at all. Current restoration models are trained on noisy footage and use that texture. Heavy denoising first leaves them less to work with and makes skin look waxy.
